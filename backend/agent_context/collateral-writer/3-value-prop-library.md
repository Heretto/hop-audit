# Value prop library

Heretto's worked examples of why structured content (content strategy plus a CCMS) is critical to a business, from the offsite value-prop workshop.

**This is context, not a menu.** It shows how the team thinks about business consequences and gives you vocabulary, roles, and real examples (regulations, cases). Write the business case for each prospect from its own findings and industry. If an example here fits, use it, and keep the team's wording where you borrow a line; if none fits, write a new one. Only claim what the audit's findings support for this prospect.

The list groups examples under Risk, Customer Acquisition, Customer Retention, Margin (the Cost imperative), and AI. AI is not a separate imperative on the one-sheet; AI examples land under Risk or Cost (see `business-imperatives.md`).

Format for each: the situation; **So what:** the consequence; **Who cares:** the role that owns it; **Applies to:** industries; **One sentence:** *For [industry], structured content is critical to [imperative] because without it, [consequence].*

## Workshop examples

For medical device manufacturers selling in the EU, structured content is critical to managing risk because without it, they can’t prove which version of their instructions for use shipped with which device, and a failed audit can suspend their CE certificate and halt EU sales.

For SaaS companies with freemium or free-trial tiers, structured content is critical to customer acquisition because without it, users can’t get set up on their own, stall before seeing value, and never convert to paid, so marketing spend fills the funnel but revenue doesn’t follow.

## Risk

**Regulatory compliance and audit.** In regulated industries, documentation is part of what gets approved, and the company must prove which version shipped with which product.
So what: the company can lose the right to sell the product. For medical devices, that means FDA inspection findings (Form 483), warning letters, product holds, or recalls, and in the EU, suspension or withdrawal of the CE certificate. For pharma, labeling that doesn’t match the approved version can be treated as misbranding, exposing the product to seizure and the company to injunction. Across industries, a failed audit triggers remediation and closer scrutiny of future submissions.
Who cares: Head of Regulatory Affairs, VP of Quality, Chief Compliance Officer, CEO.
Applies to: medical device, pharma, biotech, and diagnostics companies, and any company whose products require regulatory clearance or approval.
One sentence: For medical device, pharma, and diagnostics companies, structured content is critical to managing risk, because without it they can’t prove which approved version shipped with which product, and they can lose the right to sell.

**Certification and delivery obligations.** An aircraft or component type-certificate holder must provide Instructions for Continued Airworthiness (14 CFR 21.50). Defense programs require S1000D-compliant technical data as a contract deliverable.
So what: the product can’t be certified or accepted, and payment milestones tied to delivery are withheld. The company is also disqualified from bidding on programs that require the standard.
Who cares: VP of Product Support, program managers, head of contracts, CFO.
Applies to: aerospace manufacturers and suppliers, defense contractors, and companies selling into government programs with mandated documentation standards.
One sentence: For aerospace and defense manufacturers, structured content is critical to managing risk, because without it products can’t be certified or accepted, and payments tied to delivery are withheld.

**Product liability.** Instructions and warnings are legally part of the product.
So what: an outdated warning in one document variant becomes a failure-to-warn claim. Without an audit trail showing when the warning changed and where it was published, the company can’t defend itself, and one incident can mean litigation, settlements, and higher insurance costs.
Who cares: General Counsel, Chief Risk Officer, head of product safety, CFO.
Applies to: manufacturers of products that can injure people or damage property, including industrial equipment, machinery, automotive, medical devices, and consumer products.
One sentence: For manufacturers of equipment, machinery, and consumer products, structured content is critical to managing risk, because without it one outdated warning becomes a failure-to-warn claim the company can’t defend.

**Market access.** The EU requires safety and use information in each member state’s local language, and the European Accessibility Act requires accessible documentation for covered products and services.
So what: the product can’t legally be sold in that market. Regulators can order it withdrawn, and competitors who can comply take the share.
Who cares: Chief Commercial Officer, GM of international or regional business, Head of Regulatory Affairs, CEO.
Applies to: manufacturers of physical products, medical device companies, and consumer electronics and digital service providers selling into the EU or other multilingual regulated markets.
One sentence: For manufacturers and service providers selling into the EU, structured content is critical to managing risk, because without it they can’t meet local-language and accessibility requirements and can’t legally sell in those markets.

## Customer Acquisition

**Freemium and product-led conversion.** In freemium and product-led models, nobody from sales or CS is involved until a user decides to pay. Self-serve content is what gets users to activate and see enough value to upgrade.
So what: users stall before activation and leave without anyone noticing. Free-to-paid conversion falls, customer acquisition cost rises because more free users are needed per paying customer, and revenue growth becomes capped by content quality rather than by demand.
Who cares: Head of Growth, Chief Product Officer, CRO, CFO.
Applies to: SaaS companies with freemium, free-trial, or product-led growth models.
One sentence: For freemium and product-led SaaS companies, structured content is critical to winning customers, because without it users stall before activation and never convert to paid.

**API and developer-first companies.** The documentation is the product’s interface, and developers evaluate by trying to integrate.
So what: developers pick a competitor during evaluation, before sales is ever involved. Pipeline dries up at the top of the funnel, and the cause never shows up in CRM data.
Who cares: CTO, Chief Product Officer, head of developer relations, CRO.
Applies to: API-first companies, developer platforms, infrastructure software, SDK vendors, and integration platforms.
One sentence: For API-first and developer platform companies, structured content is critical to winning customers, because without it developers choose a competitor during evaluation, before sales is ever involved.

## Customer Retention

**Retention and expansion in low-touch models.** In SMB and low-touch tiers, the documentation effectively is the CSM, and under usage-based pricing, adoption is revenue.
So what: customers churn at renewal, or the company pays for human support the tier’s margin can’t absorb. Features customers can’t figure out go unused, so expansion revenue never materializes.
Who cares: Chief Customer Officer, VP of Customer Success, CRO, CFO.
Applies to: SaaS companies with SMB or low-touch tiers, and any company with usage- or consumption-based pricing.
One sentence: For SaaS companies with low-touch tiers or usage-based pricing, structured content is critical to keeping customers, because without it adoption stays shallow, customers churn, and expansion never happens.

**Partner and channel delivery.** Partners can only sell and deliver what they can learn.
So what: partner implementations are inconsistent or fail, customers blame the vendor, and churn and reputation damage land on the vendor even though a partner did the work. Partners also shift effort toward vendors that are easier to sell and deliver.
Who cares: VP of Channel or Alliances, head of partner enablement, CRO.
Applies to: companies that sell or implement through resellers, distributors, systems integrators, or implementation partners.
One sentence: For companies that sell or implement through partners, structured content is critical to keeping customers, because without it partner implementations fail and the churn lands on the vendor.

## Margin (Cost)

**Cost to serve.** Self-service content absorbs questions that would otherwise become tickets, and field technicians need the correct procedure for the exact configuration in front of them.
So what: support headcount grows in step with the customer base, and gross margin compresses as the company scales. Where the company provides field service, it also means repeat visits, longer customer downtime, and SLA penalties.
Who cares: VP of Support or Customer Service, VP of Field Service, COO, CFO.
Applies to: companies with large customer bases and high support volume, and equipment and hardware manufacturers with field service organizations.
One sentence: For companies with large customer bases or field service organizations, structured content is critical to protecting margin, because without it support costs grow in step with the customer base.

**Localization cost.** Translation is billed per word, and reuse means each segment is translated once.
So what: the company pays to translate the same content repeatedly across every variant and language. Translation spend grows with every new product and market, and translations drift out of sync, which feeds back into the compliance and market-access risks above.
Who cares: head of localization or globalization, GM of international business, CFO.
Applies to: global manufacturers, medical device companies, and software companies that document in many languages.
One sentence: For global companies documenting in many languages, structured content is critical to protecting margin, because without it they pay to translate the same content again and again.

**Variant and scale economics.** Conditional reuse separates content workload from the number of products, SKUs, and configurations.
So what: documentation headcount grows with the product line, or the content falls behind the product. Either outcome limits how fast the company can expand its offering.
Who cares: VP of Engineering or Product, COO, CFO.
Applies to: industrial manufacturers, high-tech hardware companies, and configurable software vendors with many models, SKUs, or configurations.
One sentence: For manufacturers and software vendors with many models or configurations, structured content is critical to protecting margin, because without it documentation headcount grows with every new product.

## AI

**Version and configuration precision.** Unless content is tagged by version and configuration, AI can’t tell which answer applies to which product.
So what: AI confidently gives the procedure for the wrong model. In regulated or safety contexts, that becomes a compliance violation or a safety incident, with the consequences in points 1 and 3.
Who cares: VP of Support, Head of Quality or Regulatory Affairs, Chief Product Officer.
Applies to: companies supporting multiple product versions, models, or configurations in the field at once, such as hardware, industrial equipment, medical devices, and enterprise software with long-lived releases.
One sentence: For companies supporting many product versions in the field, structured content is critical to using AI safely, because without it AI gives the right answer for the wrong product.

**Liability for AI answers.** Companies own what their AI tells customers. In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024), the tribunal rejected the airline’s argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own statements, and ruled that having the correct policy elsewhere on its website was no defense. Accurate content somewhere isn’t enough; every source the AI draws on has to be consistent and current.
So what: the company is bound by its AI’s misstatements and faces claims, refunds, and regulatory attention for answers no one approved.
Who cares: General Counsel, Chief Customer Officer or VP of Customer Experience, CIO.
Applies to: companies deploying customer-facing AI such as support chatbots and virtual agents, particularly in travel, financial services, healthcare, and other consumer-facing industries.
One sentence: For companies deploying customer-facing AI, structured content is critical to using AI safely, because without it they are bound by answers no one approved.

**A capability ceiling.** Retrieval and agentic AI depend on content broken into chunks and tagged by product, version, audience, region, and task.
So what: the company stalls at a generic chatbot while competitors with structured content ship version-aware agents and in-product guidance. The gap takes years to close, because restructuring content takes years.
Who cares: CIO, Chief AI or Chief Digital Officer, CEO.
Applies to: any company with a substantial documentation or knowledge base that plans to build AI-driven support, product guidance, or agents on it.
One sentence: For companies building AI on their documentation, structured content is critical to scaling AI, because without it they stall at a generic chatbot while competitors move ahead.

**Provenance and auditability.** Regulated buyers increasingly require AI outputs to trace back to an approved, versioned source.
So what: AI can’t be deployed in regulated workflows at all. The company either forgoes the efficiency gains or deploys anyway and fails the audit.
Who cares: Chief Compliance Officer, Head of Regulatory Affairs, CIO.
Applies to: companies operating in or selling into life sciences, financial services, aerospace, and the public sector.
One sentence: For companies in regulated industries, structured content is critical to scaling AI, because without it AI can’t be deployed in regulated workflows at all.

**AI-generated content volume.** As teams use AI to draft content, output multiplies, along with duplication, drift, and contradictions.
So what: the source content degrades faster than anyone can review it. Every system built on that content, including the AI itself, gets less accurate over time.
Who cares: head of documentation or content operations, Chief Data Officer, CIO.
Applies to: companies whose documentation, knowledge management, or content teams are adopting AI drafting tools at scale.
One sentence: For companies adopting AI drafting tools at scale, structured content is critical to using AI safely, because without it source content degrades faster than anyone can review it.

**Visibility to buyers’ AI.** Buyers and their agents use AI tools to research and shortlist vendors, and those tools read public documentation.
So what: the company is misrepresented or left out of AI-generated shortlists and loses deals it never knew it was in.
Who cares: CMO, CRO.
Applies to: B2B companies whose buyers research through public documentation, such as software, technical products, and components.
One sentence: For B2B companies whose buyers research online, structured content is critical to being found by AI, because without it they are left out of AI-generated shortlists.

**Repeated data-prep cost.** A structured source means content preparation is paid for once.
So what: every AI initiative starts with its own cleanup and restructuring project. That makes each use case slower and more expensive, and many never get past the pilot stage.
Who cares: CIO, Chief Data Officer, CFO.
Applies to: companies running or planning several AI initiatives on the same content, across support, sales enablement, product, and training.
One sentence: For companies running several AI initiatives, structured content is critical to scaling AI, because without it every initiative pays for its own content cleanup.
